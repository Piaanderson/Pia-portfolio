# Building the Team That Built the Products

---

## Hero Section

**Headline:** We grew PwC Digital's design team from 7 people to 130 in under two years. Only three left voluntarily in four years — and two of them came back.

**Subhead:** I designed the hiring, onboarding, and culture systems that let a brand-new product arm of a global consulting firm scale without losing the thing that made it worth joining.

> **Metric callout:** 3 voluntary departures over four years on a 130-person team. Two of those designers returned after realizing what they'd left.

> **Metric callout:** HR asked our team to help them model onboarding for other disciplines across the firm.

---

## The Situation

PwC Digital was a new venture inside PwC Advisory. The idea was straightforward and ambitious in equal measure: sector teams across the firm had built brilliant internal tools with commercial potential, and PwC wanted to package and sell them as products. New engagements were coming in every week. The design team, which had been seven people, needed to become something much larger, much faster, with no existing playbook for how to do it.

When I joined as a Senior Manager, there was no structured hiring process. Interviewers scheduled calls with candidates using no shared agenda and no coordination between rounds. In second interviews, candidates often answered questions they had already answered, because the teams had no system for passing context between them. Different candidates were getting meaningfully different experiences depending on who happened to show up, which is both an operational problem and a legal exposure.

There was also no formal onboarding. New designers arrived and learned the team through proximity and luck.

---

## My Role

I was promoted from Senior Manager to Director during this period and became the head of design's deputy, the second-in-command on a team that grew from 7 to 130. I created or led the creation of every system described here: the interview framework, the hiring pipeline documentation, the onboarding Miro board, the Design Ops function, and the peer review structure. As we scaled, I also helped architect a third leadership tier — Design Managers sitting beneath the Design Directors — to keep the team's structure from outpacing our ability to lead it.

---

## Decision 1: Building a hiring process that protected the culture we hadn't finished building yet

The first thing I recognized was that the ad-hoc interview process wasn't just inefficient. It was a risk. When different candidates get different questions, evaluations become inconsistent in ways that are hard to defend and easy to misread as bias. We needed structure, and we needed it before the next round of hiring, not after.

I brought the leads together to surface the questions we actually asked and why we liked them. From that conversation, we sorted the strongest questions into two distinct interview templates — one for the first round, one for the second. The first-round template lived in a shared folder. Before each call, interviewers created a copy, took notes directly in the document during the conversation, and left it behind. The second-round team opened that document before their call. They already had the candidate's answers from round one waiting in a second tab, alongside their own questions.

This sounds simple. The effect was significant. Every candidate got a consistent experience. Second-round interviewers came in prepared rather than repeating ground already covered. And we had a record of every conversation, which proved useful more than once when questions about specific candidates came up weeks later.

We also made a deliberate decision to screen hard for soft skills and cultural fit, not just portfolio strength. That choice compounded over time in ways that are hard to attribute to any single hire but showed up clearly in how the team held together under pressure.

---

## Decision 2: Designing onboarding as a product

A team growing this fast needed onboarding that could scale without requiring a senior person to personally shepherd every new joiner. I built the onboarding experience around a Miro board that used macro and micro navigation — the kind where the zoomed-out view gives you the full map, and zooming in reveals exactly what you need without losing your place.

The leads contributed brainstorming and content. I designed the structure. The board covered everything from software licenses and Mac setup to time reporting, Figma file conventions, accessibility standards, how we work with developers, and video demos of our largest products. New joiners arrived with a specific action list: first week, first month, first 60 days, first 90 days.

Before a new hire's first day, they heard from us about what to expect. On day one, a "friendlies" leader met with them — someone whose job that day was to make the new person feel welcome, walk them through the board, and answer the questions people are sometimes embarrassed to ask their direct manager in the first week.

HR later asked us to help them adapt this approach for onboarding across other disciplines. We worked with their leads rather than building it for them.

---

## Decision 3: Recognizing when the work had outgrown me

By the time the team had grown to a certain size, I was spending 60 to 70 percent of my time on hiring logistics: portfolio reviews, first-round coordination, recruiter calls, moving interview notes from the first-round team to the second, following up on pending decisions, and managing the onboarding pipeline. Other managers and senior managers were carrying load too, and we were all still drowning.

The work wasn't wrong. It just didn't require a Director. I petitioned our Partner to create a Design Ops role.

Once that person was up to speed, I shifted to reviewing portfolios and participating in second interviews alongside the other directors. She and I met two to three times a week to stay aligned on everything else. I stayed connected to every new hire and met them when they joined. The difference was that I was also, finally, able to lead the team that was already there.

That decision is the one I am most glad I made quickly. Waiting longer would have cost us.

---

## Decision 4: Keeping the culture intact as the team tripled, then tripled again

When a team is seven people, culture is ambient. Everyone is in the same conversations, the same Slack threads, the same review sessions. When a team is 130, culture is architecture. It requires deliberate construction or it disappears.

I started weekly peer review sessions early, when the whole team could still fit in one call. As we grew, those evolved into team-wide show-and-tells, and I worked with each Design Manager to stand up smaller peer reviews within their individual teams. The rhythm stayed intact; the format adapted to the scale.

We also ran sessions to define our team manifesto — who we were, what we stood for, what we expected of each other. One thing the leadership team was consistent about: we told every designer that they had our support to ask hard questions, push back on bad decisions, and escalate when their recommendations weren't being followed to the detriment of the product. The role was not to take design orders. It was to understand the problem beneath the ask, advocate for the user, build bridges with partners, and use good judgment about when to compromise and when to hold the line.

That message, repeated by every leader on the team, was part of why people stayed.

---

## Outcomes

> **Metric:** 7 to 130 designers in under two years, with a pipeline and onboarding system that scaled without breaking.

> **Metric:** 3 voluntary departures over four years. 2 of those designers returned after leaving.

> **Metric:** HR modeled their broader onboarding process on ours and asked our team to help them build it out for other disciplines.

> **Qualitative:** The team was consistently recognized across PwC Digital for its culture, which the leadership attributed directly to how carefully we hired for soft skills, how thoroughly we onboarded, and how intentionally we maintained the close-team feeling as we grew.

---

## What I Would Do Differently

I would have petitioned for the Design Ops role sooner. I waited until the cost was impossible to ignore, and by the time I made the case to our Partner, we had already lost weeks of leadership capacity to work that should never have been mine. The right time to build the operational support structure is before you feel the strain, not after.

I would also have worked earlier to document the cultural principles in a way that new Design Managers could carry forward consistently without needing direct context from me. As we added the third leadership tier, some of the nuance in how we talked about designer advocacy and escalation didn't transfer as cleanly as I would have liked. Written artifacts would have helped.
