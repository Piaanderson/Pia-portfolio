# Script Decisions Log — Capital One Portfolio Review

**Date:** September 27, 2026
**Context:** Pia is preparing a 26-slide portfolio review for the Product Design Director, Card Servicing & Support Platform Design role at Capital One (R242718). The presentation is on Tuesday, September 29. Two case studies: Southwest Airlines Ops Suite and Project Forge. Format is 20 min presenting + 10 min Q&A per case study, ~60 minutes total.

This document captures the suggestions made, Pia's responses, and the rationale behind each decision. Use this as the thinking layer when applying these changes to the slide deck.

---

## 1. Name service design explicitly

**The suggestion:** The JD says "We use service design and systems thinking to transform backend complexity into seamless, highly intuitive solutions." The script demonstrated systems thinking beautifully but never used the phrase "service design." The Forge experience-layer strategy IS service design — mapping the full journey across three backend systems, understanding organizational ownership as a design constraint. The Southwest NOC work IS service design — understanding operational context, shift patterns, information flows across roles. Name it at least once so the panel hears that you speak their language. They'll be listening for it.

**Pia's response:** Yes, please help add that.

**Where it landed in the script:** Origin story (Slide 5). After describing the PwC internal tools work: "It's service design applied to internal tools — mapping the full workflow, understanding the operational context, and designing experiences that make complex work feel simple."

**Why it matters for the deck:** If the origin story slide has any supporting text or visual framing, the service design language should be visible or at least supported by the visual treatment. This isn't a throwaway line — it's a vocabulary signal to the panel.

---

## 2. Trim the About Me section

**The suggestion:** 3-4 minutes of intro in a 60-minute presentation is a lot. The personal slide (Slide 3) is warm and lovely, but 45 seconds on hair dye, dogs, Pilates, and kids' school activities is time that could go toward the work. Trim to 25-30 seconds. The interviewers don't need to know about hair dye — the family photo does the "I'm a real human" work visually. Keep UXPA and teaching AI at schools. The origin story meanders through "tried other assignments, friend told me about PwC" — cut the middle and go straight from Southwest to PwC internal tools. The emotional core — "I got to make people's days easier" — is the gold.

**Pia's response:** All solid suggestions to lighten that area.

**What changed:**
- Slide 3: Removed the hair dye / "the sunset is intentional" line. Kept everything else (family, hobbies, UXPA, teaching AI at schools).
- Slide 5: Cut "I left Southwest, tried a few other assignments, and knew I wanted to get back to internal-facing work. A friend interviewed for a role on PwC's tax technology team and told me it sounded like me. He was right." Went straight from the Southwest emotional moment to "I've spent the years since doing the same kind of work at PwC."

**Why it matters for the deck:** Slide 3 may need minor text adjustment if the hair dye line appeared on-slide. Slide 5's visual (if it has a career timeline) needs updating for the new arc.

## 3. Fix the career timeline

**The suggestion (came up during editing):** The script said "I started my career at American Airlines" — but that's not accurate. AA was roughly 10 years into Pia's career and her first leadership role.

**Pia's response:** "I didn't actually start my career at American Airlines. When I was at American Airlines, that was my first leadership role. I had already worked in an agency for five years doing advertising that evolved into user experience for landing pages. Then I did freelancing and ran my own small agency for about three years working with local small businesses and franchise owners — websites, web presence, social media, advertising. Then I came to American Airlines to modernize their web experience. That was already ten years into my career."

**Pia's preferred phrasing:** "The first part of my career was in agencies — advertising that evolved into UX — then freelancing and running my own small shop building web presence for local businesses and franchise owners."

**What changed:** Origin story now reads: "The first part of my career was in agencies — advertising that evolved into UX — then freelancing and running my own small shop building web presence for local businesses and franchise owners. American Airlines was my first leadership role, modernizing their web experience. From there I went into consulting, and my first assignment was Southwest."

**Why it matters for the deck:** If Slide 5 has a career timeline visual, it needs to be: **Agencies → Freelance → AA → Consulting/SWA → PwC** (not starting at AA).

## 4. Name "Navigate Nascent Domains" explicitly

**The suggestion:** This is literally the first bullet in the JD's role expectations: "Navigate Nascent Domains: Take the lead on defining the design strategy for high-priority, emerging business areas where the 'target state' isn't yet known." Forge IS this story, but it was framed primarily as a leadership story. Explicitly name the ambiguity: "When I started, there was no product spec, no design brief, no clear end state." THAT is navigating a nascent domain. Name it as such. The panel is checking a mental rubric — make it easy for them.

**Pia's response:** Yes, please help add that.

**What changed:** Slide 16 (Forge title card) spoken content now includes: "When I started, there was no product spec, no design brief, no clear end state — three legacy systems, a stakeholder vision that was half-formed, and a mandate to show something compelling fast."

**Why it matters for the deck:** This is spoken content, not on-slide text. The slide itself stays the same (Forge dashboard hero, title, subtitle). But the framing matters for how the slide sets up the story.

## 5. Add pilot metrics

**The suggestion:** Capital One is aggressively data-driven — "data and research insights" appears multiple times in the JD, and they expect "proficiency in business case development." Southwest metrics are great. Forge metrics are necessarily thin because it's in pilot. But not mentioning what you're measuring is a gap. Even expected metrics show you think about measurement as a design practice.

**Pia's response:** She provided specific metrics they're tracking:
- Time to budget creation for sample projects
- Reduction of duplicates sent to deployment
- Quantitative measurements on user satisfaction with multiple draft budget comparison in one project (vs. old way of creating duplicate projects to compare)
- Quantitative measurements on user satisfaction with Partner pursuit dashboard helpfulness in deciding which opportunities to pursue, why, and suggested angles and talking points

**What changed:** Slide 24 (Outcomes) now opens with: "The project is in pilot, and we're tracking the metrics that matter: time to budget creation for sample projects, reduction in duplicate projects sent to deployment, user satisfaction with the multi-draft comparison versus the old duplicate-project workflow, and whether the Partner pursuit dashboard's ranked signals and suggested angles are actually helping teams make better pursuit decisions."

On-slide text also added a new line: **Tracking:** Time to budget creation · Duplicate project reduction · User satisfaction (multi-draft comparison + pursuit dashboard)

**Why it matters for the deck:** Slide 24 needs a new visual element — the "Tracking" line should appear on the slide with a slightly different treatment to distinguish "what we're measuring" from "what we've achieved."

## 6. Add Influence Through Education

**The suggestion:** The JD has a standalone bullet: "You will frequently work with partners and technical stakeholders who are new to the design process. Success requires the ability to 'teach' design value while simultaneously delivering." The script touches this in the Forge stakeholder story but never names it as a skill. The Southwest SOD story could carry this too — Pia was introducing user-centered design to a domain that had never had a designer in the room.

**Pia's response:** Sure, that sounds good — that's most projects and most teams, it's a good skill to have and one at which she's fairly strong.

**What changed:** Added to Slide 13 (Research & Growing the Team) spoken content: "In both of these environments, part of the job was helping non-design partners understand why the design process matters — not just what it produces. The SODs had never worked with a designer. The engineers hadn't seen user research drive architecture decisions. Teaching design value while delivering design work has been a constant across my career."

**Why it matters for the deck:** This is spoken content. The slide visual (tarmac tablet photo) stays the same. No on-slide text change needed.

## 7. Add accessibility

**The suggestion:** Pia has the IAAP certification on Slide 4, but accessibility is never mentioned again. For internal tools at a regulated financial institution, a11y isn't nice-to-have — it's table stakes. One sentence in the design system section would differentiate her. The certification becomes evidence rather than a bullet point.

**Pia's response:** Absolutely — accessibility is at the heart of what she does.

**What changed:** Added to Slide 12 (Design System Part 2) spoken content: "Accessibility was non-negotiable from the start — keyboard navigation, focus management, and WCAG AA contrast were baked into every component. In a 24-hour operations center, accessible design isn't a compliance checkbox. It's the difference between a tool people can use for a 12-hour shift and one they can't."

**Why it matters for the deck:** A small accessibility callout should appear on Slide 12 as a supporting visual element: "WCAG AA · Keyboard navigation · Focus management — built into every component." Understated, not a headline.

## 12. Rework "Why are you leaving PwC?" Q&A answer

**The suggestion:** Capital One values humility. The original answer was generic and forward-looking but lacked authenticity. Suggested adding "I'm proud of what we've built at PwC" before "I'm looking for a role where I can go deep."

**Pia's response:** She pushed back on the softening — doesn't think it belongs. Her real reason: "Our work now consists of creating experiences sold to clients whose users we're never able to really understand, rather than for internal PwC users. While it's still internal product work, optimizing workflows for people we can't meet isn't the type of experience design I'm passionate about."

**What changed:** Q&A answer rewritten to reflect her authentic reason: "I've had a great run at PwC — building teams, shipping products, leading transformations. But our work has evolved toward creating experiences sold to clients, and I find myself designing for users I'll never meet. What drew me to this career was designing for the people in the room — the supervisors, the agents, the teams working the tools every day. Capital One's Card Servicing & Support role is exactly that: internal platforms for real operational users whose work you can observe, whose feedback you can hear, and whose days you can make better. That's the kind of design leadership I want to do."

**Why it matters for the deck:** This is Q&A prep only — no slide impact. But the answer is significantly stronger than the original because it directly connects her motivation to the specific role.

## Text density note

**Pia flagged:** The previous version of the deck is too text-heavy on certain slides, especially Slides 8 (My Role & The Suite) and 13 (Research & Growing the Team). She'll be talking while showing these slides — the audience shouldn't be reading long paragraphs.

**The direction:** Don't strip text entirely — the deck should still have personality and design. Just make text-heavy slides more scannable: shorter lines, compact bullets, breathing room. The images should do more work on those slides, with text supporting rather than duplicating the spoken narrative.
